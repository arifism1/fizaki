Interior designers lose more to unqualified consultations than to missed calls, because a single mismatched site visit consumes half a day that a real client could have had. The solution is to ask three qualifying questions — property type, timeline and budget band — automatically at first contact, before anything reaches your calendar.

## Key takeaways

- Your scarcest resource is **your time**, not your leads.
- Three questions asked at first contact — **property, timeline, budget band** — remove most mismatches.
- Stating a **starting price band publicly** filters better than any qualifying script.
- The second big leak is the **quote that goes silent**; a structured follow-up recovers a meaningful share.
- Automated qualification is politer than a human doing it, because it happens instantly and without judgement.

---

## The real problem is not lead volume

Most designers have enough enquiries. What they do not have is a filter.

The typical week:

- Someone messages on Instagram after seeing a project
- You reply, they seem keen
- You schedule a call, then a site visit
- You drive across the city and spend two hours measuring, listening and sketching ideas
- You send a proposal
- Silence — their budget was a fraction of your minimum, and they were never going to proceed

That is most of a working day, gone. Do it twice a week and you have lost a quarter of your billable capacity to people who were never clients.

The honest framing: **an unqualified consultation is not a lost lead, it is a stolen day.**

## The three questions that filter almost everything

Ask these at first contact, before any call is scheduled.

### 1. Property type and size

*"Is this a 2BHK, 3BHK, villa or commercial space? And roughly how many square feet?"*

This sets the scale immediately and tells you whether the project is even in your range.

### 2. Timeline

*"When are you hoping to start — within a month, in 2–3 months, or still exploring?"*

"Still exploring" is not a rejection. It is a **nurture** lead, not a **visit** lead. The mistake is treating both identically. Someone six months out gets added to a slow-drip sequence, not your Saturday.

### 3. Budget band

This is the one designers avoid, and it is the most important. Never ask "what is your budget?" — people either do not know or refuse to say. Give bands:

> *"To make sure we're the right fit, which range are you working within?*
> 

> *• Under ₹5 lakh*
> 

> *• ₹5–15 lakh*
> 

> *• ₹15–30 lakh*
> 

> *• ₹30 lakh+*
> 

> *• Not sure yet"*
> 

Selecting a band is far easier than naming a number, so response rates are high. And it works in both directions — it also identifies the client with a larger budget than you assumed.

**"Not sure yet" is a valid answer**, not a disqualification. It signals someone who needs education, so send them your typical project cost ranges and let them self-select.

## Publish a starting price. It filters harder than any script.

Most Indian designers refuse to publish any figure, fearing it scares people off. It does — and that is precisely the point. It scares off the wrong people before they consume your time.

A single line on your website does most of the filtering for you:

> *"Our full-home interior projects typically start from ₹8 lakh for a 2BHK."*
> 

The people who leave were never going to hire you. The people who stay have pre-qualified themselves and arrive at the call already comfortable with the range — which makes the conversation about design rather than about price.

## The second leak: the quote that goes silent

You send a detailed proposal. Nothing. You do not chase, because it feels desperate.

But silence after a proposal is rarely rejection. It is usually a spouse to consult, a possession date that moved, or a comparison with two other studios.

A structured sequence, sent automatically:

| When | Message |
| --- | --- |
| Day 2 | "Just confirming the proposal reached you — happy to walk through anything." |
| Day 5 | A relevant completed project of similar scope and budget |
| Day 10 | "Where are you in your thinking? No rush — just want to hold the timeline if you're moving ahead." |
| Day 21 | "Closing this off for now — do reach out whenever the time is right." |

Four messages, useful rather than needy. This recovers proposals that would otherwise die silently, and it costs nothing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I qualify interior design leads without being rude?

Ask about property type, timeline and budget band at first contact, framed as making sure you are the right fit. Offering budget ranges to choose from rather than asking for a number makes it comfortable to answer, and automating it means the question arrives instantly and impersonally rather than mid-conversation.

### Should interior designers publish their prices?

Publishing a starting range is usually beneficial. It filters out prospects far below your minimum before they consume a consultation, and the clients who do enquire arrive already comfortable with your range, which shifts the first conversation from price to design.

### How do I ask a client about their budget?

Offer bands rather than an open question. Most people cannot name a figure for a project type they have never commissioned, but almost everyone can select a range. Include a "not sure yet" option and send those prospects typical project costs so they can self-assess.

### What should I do about clients who go silent after a proposal?

Follow up in a structured, useful sequence rather than not at all. A confirmation on day two, a relevant past project on day five, a gentle status check on day ten and a graceful close on day twenty-one recovers a meaningful share of proposals without appearing desperate.

### Is automating enquiries suitable for a design studio?

Yes, for the administrative layer. Automation handles the first reply, qualification, scheduling and follow-up. The design conversation, concept presentation and creative direction remain entirely human, which is exactly where a studio's value lies.
